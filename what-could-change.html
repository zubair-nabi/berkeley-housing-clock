The premise

Berkeley is not slow to approve. It clears an apartment building in about six months, faster than most of California, and has permitted a higher share of its state allocation than the median of the 539 jurisdictions doing the same exercise. What is slow is everything after approval. The levers Council reached for in April 2026 were all cost levers, and the City's own consultant put the feasibility gap on a ten-unit condominium at sixteen points of return while finding that removing the in-lieu fee entirely closes two to five. RAND, measuring 140 completed projects, found time to be the dominant cost driver and priced a month of California predevelopment at roughly $1,284 per home. If that is right, the levers still unpulled are the ones that shorten time and reduce uncertainty.

01

Publish what the new permit system will already measure

On 20 January 2026 Council awarded Clariti Cloud $5,359,128 to replace Accela, over a twenty-month implementation. The Council report says the system will deliver "compliance with State laws (e.g. AB 2234 electronic submittal and permit tracking requirements)" and that it is "necessary to improve transparency and reduce permit processing times."

The cheap path is already in the specification. Section 4.14 asks for "API integration with GIS" and "the ability to drill down from spatial maps into the Permitting system to view open, expired, or pending permits." Berkeley's ArcGIS is already publicly queryable. If permit data is going to flow there anyway, public visibility is a decision about which layer is public, not a new integration.

Cost close to nothing. The Council item does not mention either and the RFP body does not contain them, but three appendices were not examined.

02

Make local funding commitments earlier and more certain

Where the State's file gives a reason for a project stalling at all, it is overwhelmingly money: 2,390 homes across 16 sites, 42% of the waiting room, are recorded as awaiting public financing. But "not enough money" is only half of it. The shape of the money matters too.

Terner Center, 691 LIHTC projects in California, 2020–2023. The association is $20,460 per unit per additional source, driven by staffing, legal and compliance overhead and by the longer timelines that stacking creates. Bars extrapolate that average linearly, which the study does not claim.

Before

Housing Trust Fund revenue fell from $10.7m in FY24 to $831,000 in FY25, against a five-year average near $9m.

$22.6m of projected in-lieu revenue sits in the pipeline, none payable until certificate of occupancy.

Projects assemble many small sources, each adding cost and time.

After

Commitments issued earlier and timed to state funding rounds, so a project carries a firm local letter into a competitive application.

Consolidated into fewer awards where the fund can bear it.

Fewer sources per project, which is itself a cost reduction.

Honest limit at roughly $9m a year against a LIHTC median development cost near $250,000 a unit, Berkeley cannot be a large single source. That is perhaps 36 fully funded homes a year. The real lever here is certainty and timing, not size, and claiming otherwise would be the weakest thing on this page.  Wrong if the Housing Trust Fund already commits early and firmly, which is internal practice not visible from outside.

03

Preapproved plans for accessory units and middle housing

Accessory dwelling units are the quiet majority of Berkeley's permitting workload: 801 separate filings produced 827 homes between 2018 and 2025, about 103 homes a year, steady across eight years and 18.3% of every home the city permits. Each one is plan-checked individually. Middle Housing is new, so duplex and fourplex volume should rise on the same path.

These are the one part of the pipeline where approval is the whole clock. For projects under five homes the wait from approval to building permit is zero: the same day, across 572 projects. There is no downstream queue to fix. Whatever time a small project loses, it loses at the front, and that is the only place an intervention can act.

Before

801 individual plan checks over eight years for what are largely repeated building types.

3.5 months to approval on the smallest projects in the city.

Design and architectural cost carried in full by every applicant.

After

A library of preapproved designs. Choosing one drops the applicant into a near-ministerial lane.

Roughly 100 reviews a year of staff capacity returned to the 5+ pipeline, where approval takes 5.9 months.

Design cost falls most on the projects least able to carry it.

Also AB 748 would require such programmes and a 30-day decision. Building one now is cheaper than complying late.  Wrong if Berkeley's ADU approvals are already effectively ministerial and the 3.5 months is applicant-side rather than City-side. Worth asking Planning before asserting it.

What this page deliberately does not say

It does not argue for lowering the inclusionary requirement, and the evidence here would not support that if it did. Berkeley's own consultant found fee relief closes only a small share of the feasibility gap, and the in-lieu fee is the Housing Trust Fund's main dedicated income.

It does not claim these three would restart the stalled projects. Interest rates and construction costs are larger forces sitting entirely outside the City's control.

And it does not treat the City as the villain. Berkeley approves faster than most of California and permits more of its allocation than most, which is on the main page too, because it is true and because it cuts against the argument on this one.
